Between annihilation and agreement:
Civil war termination in Sri Lanka and Nepal

Written during my first year at McGill University, April 2026

In May 2009, the Sri Lankan army announced the death of Velupillai Prabhakaran, leader of the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam, ending twenty-six years of civil war in a total military victory. Three years earlier, the Nepalese government and the Communist Party of Nepal signed a comprehensive peace agreement that ended ten years of insurgency entirely through negotiation. Two Asian conflicts, two radically distinct closures. Why would one war end with annihilation while the other end with political peace?

This essay argues that the divergent outcomes reflect the interaction of three core factors: the ethnic versus ideological nature of each dispute, the military balance that produced a growing asymmetry in one case and a prolonged impasse in the other, and the internal political forces that closed or opened space for negotiation.

I. The nature of the conflict

The nature of a civil war — whether rooted in ethnic identities or ideological objectives — largely shapes whether an agreement is viable. Ethnic civil wars generate specific dynamics that make agreements fragile: disputes are often territorially indivisible, violence reactivates group identities, and recruitment occurs within communities, eliminating appeals that cross boundaries.

In Sri Lanka, these dynamics were highly evident. The conflict was, from its beginning, an identity struggle between the Sinhalese majority and the Tamil minority. The LTTE embodied this logic — the creation of a Tamil separatist and independent state. Any compromise short of independence amounted to a betrayal of the movement's founding purpose.

Nepal's conflict, by contrast, did not have ethnicity as its main driver. The Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) was founded with a class-based ideological program. Ideological wars are almost always fought for control over an existing state, which makes them more amenable to solutions that distribute power within a unified structure.

II. Military balance and the logic of stalemate

In Nepal, the conflict created precisely the kind of stalemate that conflict scholars link to negotiated agreements. Neither side could win decisively. This deadlock made negotiation rationally attractive for both sides.

Sri Lanka followed a radically different logic. The Rajapaksa regime made a deliberate decision to annihilate the LTTE regardless of cost. By 2008, the military balance had shifted decisively. All attempts at mediation failed — not due to incompetence, but because the structural conditions for a deal were absent.

III. Political trends and the space for negotiation

In Nepal, the junction of a weak monarchy, marginalized political parties, and a popular revolution created conditions for an improbable coalition. Ironically, repression created the political opening that ended the war.

In Sri Lanka, the political system was structured around ethnic outbidding — competition for the Sinhalese majority vote through hardline positions toward the Tamils. Any concession was political suicide. The possibility of a negotiated coalition simply did not exist.

What Sri Lanka and Nepal demonstrate is that the disposition to negotiate is produced, manufactured, or destroyed by the conflict's structure, the balance of forces, and the political possibilities that the internal system allows.
Development Theory

Two lenses, one problem:
Modernization and dependency theory

Written during my first year at McGill University, February 2026

The debate between modernization and dependency theory cannot be reduced to choosing the better option. Rather, it requires comprehending how each perspective illuminates — and eclipses — distinct aspects of underdevelopment.

Modernization: the internal logic

Modernization theory emerged among Western policymakers in the 1950s during the Cold War. It produced a dichotomy of "traditional" versus "modern" societies — the Global South would need to adopt Global North institutions and values to develop. The theory recognizes that values, culture, and education matter, offering concrete recommendations. Yet it ignores power dynamics, colonial history, and center-peripheral relations — a serious methodological failure.

Dependency: the structural response

Dependency theory emerged in the 1960s and 1970s as a reaction from African and Latin American nations to imperialism. It holds that Global South countries were conditioned by the exploitation of dominant countries. Underdevelopment and development are aspects of the same phenomenon.

Yet dependency also presents limitations. It risks falling into structural determinism — underestimating the capacity for internal agency and change from within.

Both perspectives offer valuable but partial insights. The persistence of global inequalities suggests that internal factors and external structures both shape development trajectories.
