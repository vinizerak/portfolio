Between annihilation and agreement:
Civil war termination in Sri Lanka and Nepal

Written during my first year at McGill University, April 2026

In May 2009, the Sri Lankan army announced the death of the leader of the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam, Velupillai Prabhakaran, ending twenty-six years of civil war in a total military victory. Three years earlier, from only a few thousand kilometres away, the Nepalese government and the Communist Party of Nepal signed a comprehensive peace agreement that ended ten years of insurgency entirely through negotiation. Two Asian conflicts, two radically distinct closures. Why would one war end with annihilation while the other end with political peace?

The conventional response appeals to the intensity of the conflict or to the leaders' personal desires. This essay argues that this explanation is insufficient. The divergent outcomes instead reflect the interaction of three core factors: the ethnic versus ideological nature of each dispute, the military balance that produced a growing asymmetry in one case and a prolonged impasse in the other, and the internal political forces that closed or opened space for negotiation.

I. The nature of the conflict

The nature of a civil war — whether rooted in ethnic identities or ideological objectives — largely shapes whether an agreement is viable. Ethnic civil wars generate specific dynamics that make agreements fragile: disputes are often territorially indivisible, violence reactivates group identities, and recruitment occurs within communities, eliminating appeals that cross boundaries.

In Sri Lanka, these dynamics were highly evident. The conflict was, from its beginning, an identity struggle between the Sinhalese majority and the Tamil minority. The Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam, founded in 1976, embodied this logic — the creation of a Tamil separatist and independent state. The Tamil Eelam became a non-negotiable objective. Any compromise short of independence amounted to a betrayal of the movement's founding purpose.

Nepal's conflict, by contrast, did not have ethnicity as its main driver. The Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) was founded in 1996 with a class-based ideological program. Ideological wars are "almost always fought for control over an existing state," which makes them more amenable to solutions that distribute power within a unified structure. A party that wishes to take over the state can, in principle, accept taking part in it — as long as conditions are favourable.

II. Military balance and the logic of stalemate

The military trajectory of a civil war determines whether both parties perceive a negotiated exit as better than continued combat. In Nepal, the conflict between CPN(M) and the Royal Nepalese Army created precisely the kind of stalemate that conflict scholars link to negotiated agreements. Neither side could win decisively. This deadlock made negotiation rationally attractive for both.

Sri Lanka followed a radically different logic. The Mahinda Rajapaksa regime, elected in 2005, made a deliberate decision to annihilate the LTTE regardless of economic, human, and diplomatic costs. By 2008, the military balance had shifted decisively against the LTTE. All attempts at mediation failed — not due to incompetence, but because the structural conditions for a deal were absent.

III. Political trends and the space for negotiation

In Nepal, the junction of a weak monarchy, marginalized political parties, and a popular revolution created conditions for an improbable coalition. The king had shut all electoral channels. This logic led to a deal between the CPN(M) and seven major opposition parties in November 2005. Each side needed the other to overthrow the king. Ironically, repression created the political opening that ended the war.

In Sri Lanka, there was no such dynamic. The political system was structured around "ethnic outbidding" — competition between parties for the Sinhalese majority vote through hardline positions toward the Tamils. Any concession was political suicide. The possibility of a coalition simply did not exist.

The dominant literature tends to treat military and political elements as analytically separate variables. What Sri Lanka and Nepal demonstrate is that these domains are inseparable. The disposition to negotiate is produced, manufactured, or destroyed by the conflict's structure, the balance of forces, and the political possibilities that the internal system allows.

When these three elements converge in the direction of exclusion — as in Sri Lanka — the war ends by force. When they converge toward openness — as in Nepal — a deal becomes possible, even if imperfect.

Development Theory

Two lenses, one problem:
Modernization and dependency theory

Written during my first year at McGill University, February 2026

The debate between modernization and dependency theory cannot be reduced to choosing the better option. Rather, it requires comprehending how each perspective illuminates — and eclipses — distinct aspects of underdevelopment. While the level of analysis in the modernization perspective is behavioral, focusing on individuals while undermining structural complexities, the dependency perspective is more structural, capturing global power dynamics while neglecting the variability of human behavior.

Modernization: the internal logic

Modernization theory emerged among Western policymakers and scholars in the 1950s during the Cold War, when Western powers were seeking to strengthen their influence in the Global South. This context produced a dichotomy of "traditional" versus "modern" societies — the Global South would need to adopt Global North institutions and values to modernize.

The modernization perspective recognizes that values, culture, and education matter for development. Countries like Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan demonstrate that certain traditional features can help rather than hinder development. This view offers concrete and practical recommendations — a genuine strength.

Yet the theory has serious methodological problems. It ignores power dynamics, global structures, and center-peripheral relations. It overlooks the history of colonialism. And it claims "universal validity" while simultaneously employing the concept of "modernity of tradition" — an internal contradiction it never resolves.

Dependency: the structural response

Dependency theory emerged in the 1960s and 1970s as a reaction from African and Latin American nations to imperialism. Drawing on Marxist theory, it holds that Global South countries were conditioned by the expansion and exploitation of dominant countries. Underdevelopment and development are aspects of the same phenomenon.

The framework considers the global system as the primary unit of analysis. Rather than attributing underdevelopment to cultural values, dependency emphasizes how economic structures and processes of exploitation shape development outcomes. It explains well the persistence of global inequality by highlighting the role of historical colonial exploitation.

Yet dependency also presents limitations. Since it emphasizes external structures, it risks falling into structural determinism — underestimating the capacity for cultural and institutional change from within. And it remains more analytical than prescriptive.

Both perspectives offer valuable but partial insights. In the contemporary world marked by interconnectedness, the persistence of inequalities suggests that internal factors and external structures both mold trajectories toward development — which highlights the contributions and limitations of both theories.
